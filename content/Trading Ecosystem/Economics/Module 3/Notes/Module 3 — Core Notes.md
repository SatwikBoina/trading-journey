# E3.2 — CPI — Consumer Price Index

**Module:** [[🟡 Econ Module 3 MOC — Inflation]]

---

## What is CPI?

**Consumer Price Index = A measure of how much prices have risen for a typical Indian household.**

Government tracks the prices of a fixed "basket" of goods:
- Food (50% weight — biggest component)
- Housing (10%)
- Fuel & Light (6.84%)
- Clothing (6.5%)
- Other: education, healthcare, transport

If this basket cost ₹1,000 last year and ₹1,065 this year:
**CPI inflation = 6.5%**

---

## Why CPI is the Most Important Inflation Measure

RBI's mandate is to control **CPI inflation** at 4%.
Every repo rate decision is based primarily on CPI.

**CPI too high → RBI raises rates → Markets fall**
**CPI falling toward 4% → RBI may cut rates → Markets rally**

---

## What's Inside India's CPI Basket

| Category | Weight |
|----------|--------|
| Food & Beverages | 45.86% |
| Pan, Tobacco | 2.38% |
| Clothing & Footwear | 6.53% |
| Housing | 10.07% |
| Fuel & Light | 6.84% |
| Miscellaneous | 28.32% |

Food's huge weight means: **Bad monsoon → food prices rise → CPI spikes → RBI forced to act.**

---

## Core Inflation vs Headline Inflation

**Headline CPI** = Everything including food and fuel (volatile — changes with monsoon, oil prices)
**Core CPI** = Headline minus food and fuel (shows underlying demand pressure)

RBI looks more closely at **Core CPI** because:
- Food prices are unpredictable (weather-dependent)
- Fuel prices are controlled partly by government
- Core reflects actual demand in the economy

---

## Where to Track

MOSPI (Ministry of Statistics) releases CPI data:
- **Monthly** — around the 12th of the following month
- Track at: mospi.gov.in or Investing.com → Economic Calendar → India CPI

# E3.6 — Demand-Pull Inflation

**Module:** [[🟡 Econ Module 3 MOC — Inflation]]

---

## The Simple Explanation

**"Too much money chasing too few goods."**

When the economy is booming:
- People have more income
- Companies are hiring
- Credit is cheap and easy
- Everyone is spending

But production can't keep up with spending.
So sellers raise prices.
**That's demand-pull inflation.**

---

## India Example

During 2021–22 post-COVID recovery:
- Government had given stimulus
- Pent-up demand released
- People started buying cars, phones, homes
- Supply chains were still disrupted
- Prices shot up across categories

This was classic demand-pull inflation.

---

## RBI's Response

Demand-pull inflation = economy is overheating.
RBI raises repo rate → credit becomes expensive → people borrow and spend less → inflation cools.

This is the normal, textbook response. It works. But it slows the economy and hurts stock markets temporarily.

# E3.15 — Which Companies Benefit From Inflation (Pricing Power)

**Module:** [[🟡 Econ Module 3 MOC — Inflation]]

---

## The Key Concept: Pricing Power

**Pricing power = Ability to raise prices without losing customers.**

Companies with strong brands, moats, and essential products can pass inflation to customers.
Their margins stay protected. Their stocks hold up better.

---

## Inflation Winners in Indian Markets

### 1. Commodity Companies
Raw material prices rise → Revenue rises automatically.
Examples: Coal India, Vedanta, Hindalco, Tata Steel

Risk: When inflation falls, their margins also compress.

### 2. FMCG (Strong Brands)
Nestle raises Maggi prices 10% → People still buy Maggi.
Asian Paints raises paint prices → Painters still use Asian Paints.

Examples: HUL, Nestle, Asian Paints, Marico, Dabur
Note: Watch gross margins — if input cost rises faster than price hike, margin falls.

### 3. Real Estate Developers
Construction costs (cement, steel) rise → They raise home prices.
In inflationary periods, real assets become desirable.
Examples: DLF, Prestige, Godrej Properties

### 4. Toll Road / Infrastructure Companies
Revenue linked to traffic + escalation clauses → Inflation often baked into contracts.
Examples: IRB Infra, NHAI bonds

---

## Inflation Losers — Margin Squeeze

Companies that CANNOT pass cost increases:
- Auto ancillaries (price pressure from auto OEMs above them)
- Airlines (fuel is 30–40% of costs, cannot always raise ticket prices)
- Quick service restaurants (food costs rise, hard to raise menu prices fast)
- Companies with long-term fixed price contracts

---

## The Portfolio Strategy

High inflation period → **Overweight commodities, strong-brand FMCG, real assets**
Low/falling inflation period → **Rotate to rate-sensitive sectors (banks, autos, real estate developers)**

# E3.23 — Equities as the Best Long-Term Inflation Hedge

**Module:** [[🟡 Econ Module 3 MOC — Inflation]]

---

## The Core Truth

Over long periods, **equities beat inflation better than any other asset class.**

India data (last 20 years):
- CPI average: ~5.5% per year
- NIFTY 50 CAGR: ~12–14% per year
- Real return from NIFTY: 6–8% per year

vs

- FD return: 6–7% → After tax (30%): ~4.5% → After inflation: **negative**
- Gold return: 8–9% → After inflation: ~3% real
- Real Estate: Illiquid, hard to calculate actual CAGR

**Equities win.**

---

## Why Equities Beat Inflation

Companies are businesses. As prices rise:
1. They raise prices of their products (pass inflation to customers)
2. Revenue grows
3. Profits grow
4. EPS grows
5. Stock price grows

**A stock is a share in a real business that earns in nominal terms.**
It naturally adjusts to inflation over time.

---

## The Caveat

In the **short term**, rising inflation HURTS stocks (via RBI rate hikes).
In the **long term**, equities PROTECT against inflation.

Short-term investor: Inflation is your enemy.
Long-term investor: Inflation doesn't matter — your SIP compoundes through it.

**This is why staying invested through inflationary cycles is crucial.**
