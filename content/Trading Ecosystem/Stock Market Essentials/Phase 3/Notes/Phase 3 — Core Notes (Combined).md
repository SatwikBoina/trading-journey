# 3.1 — The Three Financial Statements Every Investor Must Know

**Phase:** [[📙 Phase 3 MOC — Fundamental Investing]]

---

## The Three Statements

Every listed company in India must publish these every quarter and year:

### 1. Income Statement (P&L — Profit & Loss)
Tells you: **Is the business making money?**
- Revenue → how much did they sell
- Expenses → how much did they spend
- Net Profit → what's left after everything

### 2. Balance Sheet
Tells you: **What does the business own and owe?**
- Assets → what the company owns (cash, machines, property)
- Liabilities → what the company owes (loans, payables)
- Equity → Net Worth (Assets minus Liabilities)

### 3. Cash Flow Statement
Tells you: **Is the profit real cash, or just accounting profit?**
- Operating Cash Flow → cash from actual business
- Investing Cash Flow → cash spent on growth
- Financing Cash Flow → cash from loans/equity

---

## The Priority Order

```
Cash Flow Statement > Income Statement > Balance Sheet
```

A company can show profit on paper but have no actual cash.
Cash flow doesn't lie. Profit numbers can be manipulated.

---

## Where to Find These

1. **Screener.in** — best for quick reading (free)
2. **Company's Annual Report** — most detailed (company website → Investors)
3. **BSE/NSE website** — all filings available

# 3.7 — PE Ratio — Am I Paying Too Much?

**Phase:** [[📙 Phase 3 MOC — Fundamental Investing]]

---

## What is PE Ratio?

**PE = Price ÷ EPS (Earnings Per Share)**

In plain English:
**"How many rupees am I paying for every ₹1 the company earns?"**

---

## Example

TCS stock price = ₹3,500
TCS EPS (last 12 months) = ₹140
TCS PE = 3500 ÷ 140 = **25x**

You are paying ₹25 for every ₹1 TCS earns.

---

## How to Interpret

| PE | Interpretation |
|----|---------------|
| <15 | Possibly cheap (or business is declining) |
| 15–25 | Fair value range for most businesses |
| 25–40 | Expensive — but may be justified if growth is high |
| >40 | Very expensive — high growth expectations priced in |

**PE is always relative. Compare to:**
1. The company's own historical PE
2. The industry/sector average PE
3. NIFTY 50 average PE (~20–22x historically)

---

## PE Trap for Beginners

Low PE ≠ always good.
A PE of 5 might mean the company is dying.

High PE ≠ always bad.
PE of 50 is normal for fast-growing tech companies.

**Context matters more than the number.**

# 3.9 — ROE — Is Management Using Money Efficiently?

**Phase:** [[📙 Phase 3 MOC — Fundamental Investing]]

---

## What is ROE?

**ROE = Net Profit ÷ Shareholders' Equity × 100**

In plain English:
**"For every ₹100 I give the company, how much profit do they generate?"**

---

## Example

Net Profit = ₹500 crore
Shareholders Equity = ₹2,500 crore
ROE = 500 ÷ 2500 × 100 = **20%**

They generate ₹20 profit for every ₹100 invested. That's good.

---

## The Benchmark

| ROE | Quality |
|-----|---------|
| <10% | Poor — look elsewhere |
| 10–15% | Average |
| 15–20% | Good |
| >20% | Excellent — management is efficient |
| >30% consistently | Exceptional — potential moat company |

---

## Indian Example Comparisons

- Asian Paints ROE: ~30%+ → Excellent
- HDFC Bank ROE: ~16–18% → Good
- Tata Steel ROE: Variable (cyclical business) → Be careful with cyclicals

# 3.15 — What is a Moat (Competitive Advantage)?

**Phase:** [[📙 Phase 3 MOC — Fundamental Investing]]

---

## The Castle Analogy

Imagine a castle. A moat (water trench) around it keeps enemies out.

In business, a **moat = something that stops competitors from stealing your customers.**

Companies with strong moats = **better long-term investments.**

---

## Types of Moats (With Indian Examples)

### 1. Brand Moat
People pay more because of the brand name.
- Asian Paints (India's most trusted paint brand)
- Nestle India (Maggi — even after the 2015 ban, people came back)

### 2. Network Effect Moat
The product becomes more valuable as more people use it.
- NPCI/UPI ecosystem
- Info Edge (Naukri — more jobs attract more candidates, more candidates attract more employers)

### 3. Cost Advantage Moat
They can produce cheaper than anyone else.
- Coal India
- Some chemical companies with proprietary processes

### 4. Switching Cost Moat
Once you use the product, switching is painful.
- Tally (accounting software — whole business runs on it)
- Banking software, hospital management software

### 5. Regulatory Moat
Government regulations protect the business.
- Stock exchanges (NSE, BSE — you can't just open a new one)
- Airport operators (AAI, GMR)

---

## How to Check if a Moat Exists

- Can a competitor copy this in 2 years? → No moat
- Is ROE consistently >20%? → Possible moat
- Has market share held for 10+ years? → Strong moat
- Do customers keep coming back without price discounts? → Moat

# 3.20 — Good Business Checklist (Pre-Buy Verification)

**Phase:** [[📙 Phase 3 MOC — Fundamental Investing]]

---

## Run This Checklist Before Every Purchase

### Business Quality
- [ ] Do I understand what this company does?
- [ ] Is the business model simple and durable?
- [ ] Does it have a competitive moat?
- [ ] Has revenue grown consistently over 5 years?
- [ ] Is profit growth consistent?

### Financial Health
- [ ] Debt/Equity < 1?
- [ ] ROE > 15% for 5 years?
- [ ] Operating margin consistent or improving?
- [ ] Positive and growing free cash flow?
- [ ] Promoter holding > 50%?
- [ ] Promoter pledging < 10%?

### Valuation
- [ ] Is PE below or near historical average?
- [ ] Is PE reasonable vs sector average?
- [ ] Is there a margin of safety?

### Management
- [ ] Does management have a track record of honesty?
- [ ] No accounting scandals or SEBI actions?
- [ ] Good capital allocation history (dividends, buybacks, or reinvestment)?

---

## The Most Important Question

**"Would I be happy owning this business for 10 years if the market closed tomorrow?"**

If yes → proceed.
If not sure → wait and do more research.
If no → move on.

# 3.32 — Position Sizing — How Much to Invest in One Stock

**Phase:** [[📙 Phase 3 MOC — Fundamental Investing]]

---

## The Core Rule

**Never put more than 10% of your portfolio in a single stock.**

For beginners, keep it at **5% per stock** until you are more experienced.

---

## Example Portfolio for ₹1,00,000

| Stock | % | Amount |
|-------|---|--------|
| Stock A | 10% | ₹10,000 |
| Stock B | 10% | ₹10,000 |
| Stock C | 8% | ₹8,000 |
| Stock D | 7% | ₹7,000 |
| ... | ... | ... |
| NIFTY 50 Index Fund | 30% | ₹30,000 |

---

## Why This Matters

If Stock A drops 50% (company fraud, sector crash):
- 10% allocation → portfolio drops only 5%
- 50% allocation → portfolio drops 25%

**Position sizing is risk management.**

---

## The Concentration Dilemma

As you get more experienced, you might concentrate more.
Warren Buffett holds very concentrated positions.

But he has 60 years of experience.
You do not. Yet.

Start diversified. Concentrate later.
